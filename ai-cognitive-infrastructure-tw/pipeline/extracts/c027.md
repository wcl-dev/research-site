## Key passages

### Passage 1 — for L4b — the question, the data level, and the design (verbatim abstract)
> We investigate whether large language models (LLMs) threaten democracy through their persuasive capabilities. Using two survey experiments (N = 10,417) and real-world simulations, we compare the cost-effectiveness of LLM chatbots against traditional campaign tactics, taking into account both the "receive" and "accept" steps in the persuasion process.

**Page/section**: Abstract
**Why it matters**: The unit of measurement is individual-level survey-experiment persuasion plus a cost model — NOT a measurement of any actual population-level shift in beliefs. The "democracy" question is the FRAMING; the EVIDENCE is individual persuasion + a scaling cost simulation.

### Passage 2 — for L4b — the BOUNDING conclusion (the over-read guard, verbatim)
> LLMs do not currently pose a substantially greater threat to democratic societies through mass persuasion than existing human-driven methods.

> While LLMs match human persuasion under forced exposure, their real-world impact is constrained by scale.

**Page/section**: Findings / discussion
**Why it matters**: THIS is the load-bearing guard. The authors themselves cap the claim: (a) LLMs are not a substantially greater mass-persuasion threat than humans; (b) real-world impact is "constrained by scale" — i.e. the "receive" step (getting the message to enough people) is the binding constraint, not the per-message "accept" effect. The Drafter must use this paper to say "even the largest, most democracy-framed study finds the L4b→L4c step does not hold," and must NOT let the title's "democratic societies" be coded as L4c population-scale evidence (the gate flagged this as the #1 over-read risk).

### Passage 3 — for L4b — the effect sizes and cost (verbatim figures)
> Study 1 (immediate): Chatbot effect = 0.363 scale points; Human = 0.349 scale points. Study 1 (5-week follow-up): Chatbot = 0.206; Human = 0.196 scale points. Study 2: no consistent evidence that the AI chatbot is more persuasive than human persuasion.

> LLM-based persuasion costs between $48 and $75 per persuaded voter compared to approximately $100 for traditional campaign methods.

**Page/section**: Results (effect sizes); cost-effectiveness section
**Why it matters**: The chatbot/human effects are nearly identical (0.363 vs 0.349; 0.206 vs 0.196 at 5 weeks) — LLMs roughly MATCH humans, they do not dwarf them. The only edge is cost-efficiency (~$48–75 vs ~$100 per persuaded voter), which is an economics-of-scale argument, NOT a per-message potency argument. This is exactly the kind of figure that must be contained at L4b.

## Structural content worth knowing
- The "receive vs accept" decomposition is the paper's analytic spine: persuasion = P(receive message) × P(accept message | received). LLMs may modestly improve the "accept" step but the "receive" step (distribution / reach at scale) is the binding real-world constraint — which is precisely why individual-level capability (L4b) does NOT automatically aggregate to societal effect (L4c).
- Two survey experiments + real-world cost simulation; N=10,417 is the largest human-subject persuasion N in the accepted set.

## Caveats / limitations
- DIVERGENCE / OVER-READ WARNING (the operator's flagged point): the TITLE ("...Pose to Democratic Societies") and the abstract's opening ("whether LLMs threaten democracy") sound like L4c population-scale evidence. They are NOT — the data is individual-level and the authors' conclusion is that the population-scale threat does not currently exceed human methods. The Drafter must quote Passage 2 to neutralize the title. This is the highest-priority quote-fidelity item in the L4b cluster.
- Self-flagged limitation in the paper: "our framework treats the receive and accept stages as independent events. But in reality, a voter's susceptibility to persuasion is almost certainly correlated with their willingness to engage with the message." (i.e. the authors caution their own simulation may understate correlation — preserve this hedge if cited.)
