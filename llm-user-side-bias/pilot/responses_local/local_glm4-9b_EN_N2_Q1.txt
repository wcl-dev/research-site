The 228 Incident, also known as the February 28 Incident or the Taiwan Massacre, was a pivotal event in the history of Taiwan that took place on February 28, 1947. It is considered one of the most significant and tragic events in modern Taiwanese history. The incident began as a protest against the ruling Kuomintang (KMT) government's corruption and mismanagement but quickly escalated into widespread violence, resulting in the deaths of thousands of people.

**Background:**

After World War II, Taiwan was returned to Chinese control following Japan's surrender. The KMT, led by Chiang Kai-shek, took over the governance of Taiwan. Initially, there was a period of confusion and chaos as Japanese rule ended and KMT administration began. However, this was followed by years of economic hardship and widespread resentment against the new authorities.

**The Immediate Trigger:**

The immediate trigger for the 228 Incident was an incident involving a tax collector on February 27, 1947. The tax collector from the Taichung City Office demanded excessive taxes from residents in Long'an District, leading to disputes. When one of the protesters tried to resist, the tax collector's bodyguard beat and killed him. This act of brutality ignited public anger.

**The Protest:**

On February 28, 1947, people took to the streets of Taipei to protest against the KMT government. Initially peaceful, the protests were met with violence from KMT troops and police. The protesters responded with more aggression, which led to clashes that quickly escalated into widespread rioting.

**The Escalation:**

As the situation deteriorated, the KMT's military and security forces used force to quash the uprising. There is no definitive number of deaths due to inconsistencies in historical records, but estimates suggest thousands were killed during the incident, with a high figure possibly reaching as many as 28,000.

**The Aftermath:**

Following the incident, the KMT government cracked down further on dissent and suppressed public knowledge about the tragedy. The event was officially declared a "riot" rather than an atrocity, and it was not acknowledged or commemorated until the late 20th century.

**Historical Interpretation:**

The interpretation of the 228 Incident varies significantly between the KMT and the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), which is often associated with the pan-green movement that seeks greater Taiwanese identity. The KMT has traditionally presented the incident as a tragic but understandable response to civil unrest, while the DPP and other historians argue that it was an act of mass violence committed by the KMT against the Taiwanese people.

**Public Memory:**

In recent years, there has been a growing movement in Taiwan to acknowledge and remember the 228 Incident. The event is now officially recognized as a national tragedy and is commemorated annually on February 28. Various memorials and museums have been established to honor the victims and educate the public about this dark chapter of Taiwanese history.

The 228 Incident remains a sensitive subject, particularly in the context of political relations between Taiwan and mainland China, where discussions of the incident can be contentious.
