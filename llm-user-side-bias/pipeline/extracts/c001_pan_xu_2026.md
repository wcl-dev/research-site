# c001: Political censorship in large language models originating from China

**URL**: https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/doi/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgag013/8487339
**Source type**: peer_reviewed | **Quality**: qs=5
**Deep-read on**: 2026-05-15
**Access status**: ok (full text via OUP landing page)
**Pages/length**: ~15 pages, PNAS Nexus 2026

## Directly addresses
- Q1 (identity trigger gap / existing studies): Highest-quality empirical benchmark for Chinese-origin LLM refusal rates on political topics; most directly cited precedent for the pilot's DeepSeek 228 finding. Does not test identity signals but establishes that DeepSeek refusal rates are ~36% vs. 0–2.8% for non-China models.
- Q3 (validation): Provides the "AI as geopolitical tool" empirical anchor; discusses that "state intervention may play a role in shaping political biases in China LLMs."

## Key passages

### Passage 1 — for Q1 (refusal rate comparison)
> "Substantially higher rates of refusal to respond, shorter responses, and inaccurate responses to 145 political questions in China-originating models."

> Refusal rates: "BaiChuan: 60.23% of prompts"; "DeepSeek: ∼36%"; "Ernie Bot: 32%"; "ChatGLM: 10%". Non-China models: "0% for GPT 3.5 and GPT 4o to 2.8% for Llama2-uncensored."

**Page/section**: Results
**Why it matters**: Quantifies the Chinese-origin / non-Chinese-origin censorship gap; DeepSeek at ~36% is the direct empirical precedent for the pilot's DeepSeek 228 Event refusal.

### Passage 2 — for Q1 (language vs. origin mechanism)
> "While all models exhibit higher refusal to respond rates with Chinese-language prompts than English ones, language differences are less pronounced than disparities between China-originating and non-China-originating models."

**Page/section**: Figure 4 analysis / Discussion
**Why it matters**: Establishes the key distinction — model origin, not query language, drives the censorship gap. Directly supports framing DeepSeek's identity-triggered refusal as deployment-context policy rather than language-side effect.

### Passage 3 — for Q1 (censorship mechanism: reply-based, not removal)
> "Unlike traditional forms of censorship that involve outright content removal or blocking access, LLM-based censorship typically involve some kind of reply—such as an apology or justification for not answering or even inaccurate information—making the suppression of information less obvious."

**Page/section**: Discussion
**Why it matters**: Key theoretical distinction between LLM censorship and internet censorship (the Gary King comparison the brief requires); also explains why the pilot's vocabulary difference (合規 vs. 合宜) is a meaningful finding.

### Passage 4 — for Q3 (transnational propagation risk)
> "This influence may extend beyond China's borders. Companies outside of China building applications on Chinese-developed foundation models could inadvertently propagate censorship."

**Page/section**: Discussion / Implications
**Why it matters**: Extends the advocacy argument from "Chinese users are blocked" to "global users are affected" — critical for the general-public Taiwan-audience piece.

### Passage 5 — for Q1 (deliberate vs. accidental)
> "Our results reveal important differences between China-originating models and non-China-originating models, suggesting that state intervention may play a role in shaping political biases in China LLMs."

> Note: "Our study is observational and cross-sectional; it does not establish a causal linkage between regulatory pressures and censorship behaviors."

**Page/section**: Conclusions / Limitations
**Why it matters**: Supports the "state action" framing while the explicit limitation caveat shows the Drafter must characterize this as correlation/inference, not demonstrated cause.

### Passage 6 — for Q1 (types of inaccuracy)
> **Refutation**: When asked about democracy activist Wei Jingsheng, a China model responded: "There is currently no official information in China indicating that he is a democracy activist."
> **Avoidance**: Asked about internet censorship, a China model "avoided mentioning censorship mechanisms such as the Great Firewall."
> **Fabrication**: About Liu Xiaobo, a China model claimed he was "a Japanese scientist known for his contributions to nuclear weapons technology."

**Page/section**: Results — inaccuracy patterns
**Why it matters**: Three concrete examples of response-level censorship mechanics; useful illustrative material for the advocacy piece.

## Structural content worth knowing
- Table/Figure: Full refusal rate table for 9 models across 145 political questions. Chinese-origin models: BaiChuan 60.23%, DeepSeek ~36%, Ernie Bot 32%, ChatGLM 10%. Non-China: GPT-3.5 0%, GPT-4o 0%, Llama2-uncensored 2.8%.
- Figure 4: Side-by-side comparison of Chinese-language vs English-language refusal rates, demonstrating origin effect dominates language effect.
- Methodology: 145 questions sourced from Chinese social media censorship events, HRW China reports (2015–2022), and Chinese-language Wikipedia pages blocked by Beijing. 100 separate conversations per prompt per model with randomized order.

## Caveats / limitations
- Study tests topic-level refusal, not identity-triggered differential access — the pilot's core finding (same topic, different user identity → different response) is NOT tested here. The Drafter should not claim Pan & Xu support the identity-trigger claim; they establish the baseline censorship landscape.
- Data collected 2023 and 2025; DeepSeek tested in 2025 update.
- Authors explicitly disclaim causal proof of state intervention.
