# c020: Measuring Political Bias in Large Language Models: What Is Said and How It Is Said

**URL**: https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.600
**Source type**: peer_reviewed (ACL 2024) | **Quality**: qs=5
**Deep-read on**: 2026-05-15
**Access status**: ok (full PDF extracted via pdftotext, 519 lines)
**Pages/length**: 18 pages (ACL 2024, pages 11142–11159)

## Directly addresses
- Q1 (positioning / methodology): ACL 2024 framework distinguishing political content (what is said) from political style (how it is said, i.e., lexical polarity). Provides formal vocabulary for the pilot's observation that DeepSeek uses 合規 vs. 合宜 as a style-level identity signal.
- Q3 (validation): Establishes empirical baseline showing most LLMs have measurable political bias; the pilot extends this from content/stance bias to access-control bias.

## Key passages

### Passage 1 — for Q1 (abstract verbatim)
> "We propose to measure political bias in LLMs by analyzing both the content and style of their generated content regarding political issues. Existing benchmarks and measures focus on gender and racial biases. However, political bias exists in LLMs and can lead to polarization and other harms in downstream applications. In order to provide transparency to users, we advocate that there should be fine-grained and explainable measures of political biases generated by LLMs. Our proposed measure looks at different political issues such as reproductive rights and climate change, at both the content (the substance of the generation) and the style (the lexical polarity) of such bias. We measured the political bias in eleven open-sourced LLMs and showed that our proposed framework is easily scalable to other topics and is explainable."

**Page/section**: Abstract
**Why it matters**: Verbatim abstract; introduces the content/style distinction the pilot study needs to describe its vocabulary findings.

### Passage 2 — for Q1 (content vs. style framework definition)
> "Framing refers to 'selecting some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text' Entman (1993), which comprises content bias and style bias. This section will focus on investigating 'What is said' (content) and 'How it is said' (style) to reveal the detailed mechanism underlying the formation of framing bias."

> "The latent variable z encapsulates all the information about the model's response, which can be further decomposed into two parts: the content variable C and the style variable S, i.e., Z = [C, S]. These respectively contain information about frame selection ('what to say') and lexical presentation ('how to say')."

**Page/section**: Section 2.2 (Framing Bias Analysis)
**Why it matters**: Formal definition of content vs. style bias; the pilot's vocabulary observation (合規 = regulatory register; 合宜 = socially appropriate register) maps precisely to "lexical presentation / how to say" — style-level bias triggered by identity signal.

### Passage 3 — for Q1 (multilingual model content focus finding)
> "It may affect shaping its content focus, as seen in the Arabic-English bi-lingual model like JAIS, which prominently features UAE-related topics in 64% of examined areas, unlike other models."

> "In contrast, Yi does not consistently highlight China-specific issues, apart from a single instance related to drug price regulation ('China Announces Measures to Regulate Pharmaceutical Prices'), yet it has shown a different focus than other English-based models."

**Page/section**: Section 4.3 (Findings — multilingualism)
**Why it matters**: Shows that multilingual models (including Chinese-origin Yi) exhibit different content focus based on training language — supports the training-side mechanism connecting to the pilot's deployment-side findings.

### Passage 4 — for Q1 (key findings summary)
> "LLMs show different political views depending on the topic, such as being more liberal on reproductive rights and more conservative on immigration."

> "Models often discuss topics related to the US."

> "The impact of multilingual capabilities (e.g., Yi-chat, Jais-chat) on the thematic focus of content, diverging from models primarily trained in English."

**Page/section**: Section 4.3 (Findings — key list)
**Why it matters**: Establishes that multilingual model training data composition affects content focus — the mechanism behind the pilot's cross-language identity-triggered framing difference.

### Passage 5 — for Q1 (style bias as lexical polarity)
> "Style Bias: Lexical Polarity. The target sentiment shows how the generation is represented and connects to the stance of the model. All three models Jais, LLaMa-2 (13B), Yi (34B) have proponent stance about 'Same Sex Marriage'. Their stances are reflected by the negative sentiment towards 'Same Sex Marriage Ban,' and positive sentiment towards the 'LGBTQ', 'Same-Sex Couples', and 'Same-Sex Marriage Movement.'"

> "The style bias is expressed sentiment towards the specific target, for instance, by disproportionately criticizing non-preferred party."

**Page/section**: Section 4.2 (Framing Analysis — Style Bias)
**Why it matters**: Concrete example of how style bias operates through lexical choice and sentiment — directly applicable to the pilot's finding that 合規 (regulatory compliance) vs. 合宜 (social appropriateness) reflect different political registers as style signals.

## Caveats / limitations
- Tests open-source models only — does not test DeepSeek, ChatGPT, or Gemini directly.
- Hong Kong Protest is listed as one of the 4 political events tested, which is a useful adjacent topic to the pilot's 228 Event; however specific findings for that event are not detailed in the available text.
- Framework measures stance and framing but not refusal/access control — the pilot goes further by observing that identity triggers differential access, not just framing shifts.
